Overview

Overview

What Manifest is, who it is for, and the one idea behind it.

What Manifest is

Manifest is a parts catalogue and job sheet for people who work with their hands. It runs on an ordinary Android phone, works with no signal, and takes its entire shape — fields, categories, colours, even the words on the buttons — from a document you control.

It comes as a pair of apps. Manifest is what the person on the tools carries. Manifest Studio is where the catalogue is built and the job forms are designed.

The one idea

Most trade software gives you fixed fields and asks you to describe your business in somebody else's vocabulary. You end up with a “Notes” column holding the three things that actually matter.

Manifest works the other way round. One document carries your field definitions, your categories, your appearance and your parts, and the app assembles itself from that document. Change the document and the app changes on every handset holding it.

A joiner browses by material and thickness. A fire contractor browses by extinguishing agent and service date. The same application, unmodified — no plugins, no configuration consultant, no per-seat fee.

It works with no signal

Signal is worst exactly where the parts are: a plant room, a basement, a loading bay, a site with no mast for a mile. Offline is not a mode you switch on — it is the normal state.

Search, browse, scan a code, open a drawing, fill in a job sheet, take a signature: all of it happens on the handset with nothing to reach. There is no spinner waiting on a server, because there is no server. Where something genuinely needs a network — fetching a catalogue update, sending a finished job — it says so, and holds the work until there is one. It tries again the next time somebody opens the app; nothing runs while the phone is in a pocket.

Who it suits

  • A trade business with a parts list, a stores area or a van stock.
  • Anyone servicing equipment on site who has to record what was done.
  • Hire businesses tracking individual assets rather than models.
  • Anyone whose data does not fit the boxes their current software provides.

It suits you less well if you need live stock quantities, scheduling and dispatch, or accounts for individual users. What it deliberately is not covers that honestly.

Getting started

Getting started

Installing the pair, the six worked examples, and your first hour.

The two apps

Manifest

For the person on the tools. Browse, search, scan, open floor plans, fill in job sheets. It reads the catalogue rather than editing it, so nobody loses a week's authoring on a busy Friday.

Manifest Studio

Everything above, plus authoring: build the catalogue, take photographs, mark up floor plans, design job forms, print labels, and hand the finished catalogue to your team.

Both can live on the same phone; in the launcher they appear as Manifest and Manifest Studio. Studio asks for the phone's PIN, pattern or fingerprint each time it opens, and will not run at all on a handset with no screen lock — it holds your whole catalogue and the key your fleet trusts.

Start from a worked example

Six complete sample catalogues ship inside the app, one per trade, each with forty parts, floor plans and assemblies. They exist to prove the claim on the front page: they look nothing like each other, and they run unchanged in the same application.

TradeShaped around
Vehicle workshopParts by manufacturer, and what they fit

Joinery and carpentryTimber by material, thickness and finish

The last is deliberately the odd one out. A hire business identifies the thing, not the model — two identical speakers are two records, and the title field is an asset tag rather than a part number. That is the same app with a different idea of what a record even is.

Choosing an example adopts it. It becomes your own catalogue under your own identity, rather than staying a shared sample. Your first five minutes are “delete what we don't stock” instead of “type in five hundred parts”.

A sensible first hour

  1. Install Manifest Studio and let it take you through the credential prompt.
  2. Load the sample nearest your trade, and browse it until the structure makes sense.
  3. Either edit that sample into your own catalogue, or import your existing spreadsheet over the top of it.
  4. Set your colours and logo and your own words for the sections.
  5. Install Manifest on a second handset and pair it.
  6. Write your recovery file before you have anything you would miss. Writing one is paid, because it takes your catalogue off the handset; checking and restoring one are free on every tier.

Step six is the one people skip. A handset is a thing that gets dropped, stolen and left on a roof, and there is no service holding a copy for you.

Your catalogue

Your catalogue

Fields, structure, your own words, appearance, and getting a catalogue built quickly.

Fields

A record carries whatever your trade needs it to. There are 44 field types, and each one knows what it is — so a date gets a date picker, a duration gets hours and minutes, money is stored as an exact amount with its currency rather than a rounded number, a phone number dials, and a link opens.

For each field you decide the label, whether it is required, whether it is searchable and how heavily it is weighted against other fields at the same rank, where it appears on the part page, and any bounds it must respect.

The full list is in the field type reference.

Structure

You decide how parts are grouped and how deep the tree goes — by category, by location, by rating, by whatever your trade actually uses to find things. A catalogue can be browsed on more than one axis at once, so the same stock can be reached by category and by where it lives.

Tapping a value on a part page — a manufacturer, a rating, a tag — narrows the list to everything sharing it, and shows as a chip you can remove. Sections are the durable version of the same idea: they come from a field's values, so the catalogue organises itself and there is no second structure to keep in step.

Your own words

The app's own section names are yours. A Build can be a Kit, an Assembly, a Rig or a Job Pack. Plans can be Sites or Drawings. A Job can be a Visit, a Service or a Call.

These are not translations. They are your nouns, carried inside your catalogue, so the identical installed app uses different words on different companies' handsets — including in menus, empty states and confirmation messages.

Appearance

Your colours and your logo travel in the catalogue, so the app on your team's phones looks like your company rather than like ours. Light and dark are both handled, and the colours are checked for contrast so a brand colour cannot make text unreadable.

Converting an existing list with AI

Studio can export a schema for AI conversion: a description of your catalogue's structure, with no parts and no prices in it.

Send that to any AI assistant along with your own parts list — in whatever shape it happens to be, however inconsistent — and it will give you back a file Manifest can import. It is the fastest route from a twenty-year-old spreadsheet to a working catalogue.

You can also export the schema alone to set up a second catalogue with the same shape as the first.

Catalogue quality

Studio can review the catalogue and report what looks wrong or unfinished — the sort of thing that is invisible while you are typing and obvious to somebody using it in a van three weeks later.

More than one catalogue

A business can hold several catalogues on one Studio — one per area of the business, or one per site — and move between them. Each is separate: its own fields, its own parts, its own appearance.

On the floor

On the floor

Browsing, search, scanning, floor plans, assemblies and your own private notes.

Browsing and the part page

Parts are listed densely, because a person holding a phone in a cold plant room wants to see ten of them, not two. Part numbers are set in a monospaced face so 0 and O cannot be confused when you are ordering one.

A part page shows every field you defined, in the order and grouping you chose, with photographs and any references to other parts.

Search

Every area has a search bar, and it sits within thumb reach rather than at the top of the screen where a one-handed grip cannot get to it.

Search covers the fields you marked searchable. Identifiers rank first — an exact match, then a match at the start, then one anywhere inside — and descriptions and other fields come after those. A weight you set breaks ties within one of those bands. Results appear as you type, and a row says which field matched.

Scanning

Point the camera at a QR code or a barcode and land directly on that part. The scanner reads 2D codes and ordinary 1D barcodes.

Scanning also fills fields in: a job sheet asking for a panel serial can have it scanned in rather than typed with gloves on. Codes a manufacturer already prints can be adopted, so you do not have to relabel stock that already carries a usable code.

Floor plans

Drop your site drawings in, mark out the zones, and place parts on the map. Tap a zone to see everything in it, and go straight from the map to a part page.

It earns its keep the first time somebody is sent to a building they have never been to, and needs to find the panel before they can start.

Builds and assemblies

Group parts into the things you actually book out or install as a unit — an assembly, a kit, a rig, a job pack. Select several parts at once and add them to a build in one action.

Doing something to several parts at once

Select a run of parts — or everything in a zone, in one action — and then act on the selection: print labels for all of them, or add them all to a build.

It is the difference between labelling a stores area in an afternoon and labelling it one part at a time.

Photographs

A part can carry photographs, taken with the phone or picked from the gallery. They are compressed on the way in and travel with the catalogue in a bundle, so a handset with no signal still has the picture.

Parts can also point at other parts, so a fitting can name what it fits and you can move between them.

My parts

Anyone can mark parts as their own favourites and attach a private note to a part — what you know about it, what it actually fits, which supplier is reliable.

Favourites and notes stay on that phone. They are not part of the catalogue and they are not sent anywhere, so an engineer's own working knowledge is theirs and does not travel with an export.

Jobs

Jobs

Designing job sheets, filling them in on site, and getting completed work off the handset.

The job sheet is a form you designed

A job form is built from the same field types as the rest of the catalogue and lives in your catalogue. Most are offered on a job — a signature, a voice note, a video clip, a photograph, a duration or an address as readily as a line of text. Two are not offered on a job yet: a status with set steps, and a choice that narrows as you pick. A form that already carries one keeps working. Whatever your trade records — visit type, devices tested, defects found, outcome, next service date, invoice total — is a field you defined, not a box we guessed at.

Templates mean a recurring visit starts half filled in. An annual service and a callback can be different forms.

Filling one in on site

Time

A timer runs while the work does, so time on site is recorded rather than remembered back at the van. It writes into a duration field you nominate, so put one on the form first, and it keeps running while the app is closed.

Photographs

Taken on the spot with the camera, or picked from the gallery, and stored on the handset with the job.

Recordings

A voice note or a video clip, through the phone's own recorder or camera app.

Signatures

Captured on the glass. Once given, a signature cannot be quietly changed — which is the entire point of having one.

Parts used

Picked from the catalogue through the same search as the rest of the app, so a fitted part resolves to a real record rather than a typed string that nobody can reconcile later.

Scanning

Serials and asset tags can be scanned straight into their fields.

Completing and reopening

A job is marked complete when the work is done. Required fields must be answered first — the form refuses rather than silently saving a half-finished record.

A completed job can be reopened if something was missed. Deleted jobs go to a bin and stay recoverable for seven days.

Getting completed work off the handset

A finished job can be sent automatically, to as many destinations as you like. You configure these once in Studio and they travel to the fleet with the catalogue, arriving working — switched on as you left them, with no step at the far end, so handsets begin sending as soon as they load the catalogue. If you want to try one van first, narrow the destination to named handsets before you publish.

Body formats for a web destination: JSON, CSV, XML, form fields or JSON lines. Sign-in is supported in several shapes, including bearer tokens, API keys, basic authentication and signed requests.

Control over what leaves, and when

  • A field mapping decides exactly which values are sent and what each is called at the other end. If a destination also attaches photographs or signatures, the screen lists those separately rather than letting a mapping imply it is the whole story.
  • Send a test sends a sample completion so you can see it arrive.
  • Show the request displays precisely what would leave the handset, before any real job does.
  • A send window holds work until working hours.
  • Wi-fi only holds work until the handset is off mobile data.
  • Named handsets can be the only ones that send to a given destination, for a pilot.

Anything not yet sent is listed plainly under Waiting to send, with the reason. Nothing is deleted for having been sent unless you asked for that explicitly.

Templates

A template is a job form with some of it already answered. An annual service, a callback, a commissioning visit and a remedial can each be their own template, so the engineer picks one at the start and begins with the right fields in front of them rather than the same blank form every time.

Templates live in the catalogue, so changing one changes it for the whole fleet at the next catalogue update.

Moving jobs by file

Jobs can be exported to a file and imported on another handset. That is the route for a business with no destinations configured at all: the work is done on a phone, the file is handed over, and it opens on the machine that needs it — with the photographs and signatures intact.

It is also how work gets off a handset that is about to be replaced.

How long jobs are kept

You can set how long completed jobs stay on a handset, and separately how long before the personal details in them are anonymised. Anonymising always comes before deleting, so a record can lose the customer's name and signature while the engineering facts stay.

Leave either empty and nothing happens on that schedule. Retention deletes for good — it is not the seven-day bin. The bin is for jobs somebody deletes by hand, which stay recoverable for seven days.

The clock does not run on work that has not been sent yet. A job waiting for a destination is not quietly deleted out from under you because a retention period elapsed.

Work arriving from elsewhere

Jobs can travel the other way. Point the app at an https address you control — your own scheduler or dispatch board — and each handset gets a Fetch row on its Jobs tab that brings down what has been assigned to it. It checks when that tab is opened; nothing polls in the background. Fetching again reconciles the list rather than duplicating it. The address, the credential and whatever comes back are yours; we never see any of it.

Labels

Labels

Printing codes that scan back to the exact record, on stock you already buy.

Printing labels

Print a QR or barcode label for a part, stick it on the shelf, the bin or the asset, and scanning it later opens that exact record.

Select any number of parts — a whole zone at once, if you like — choose your stock, and Manifest lays out the sheet and renders a PDF. It tells you how many labels, how many to a page and how many pages before it prints anything. A part with no part number has no code to print, so it is left out — and you are told how many, before any paper is spent.

Label stocks

Manifest knows the layout of 34 label stocks:

  • Plain paper — A4, A5 and Letter.
  • Avery — including L7160, L7161, L7163, L7165, L7169, L7651, 5160 and 5163.
  • Brother — the DK die-cut and continuous rolls, and TZe tape.
  • Dymo — the common LabelWriter sizes and D1 tape.
  • Zebra.

The output is an ordinary PDF, so it goes to any printer you already have, including the small thermal ones. Reprinting a single lost label does not mean reprinting the sheet.

Getting data in and out

Getting data in and out

Spreadsheet import, exports, distributing to a fleet, the catalogue server and updates.

Importing a spreadsheet

Almost every business already has its parts in a spreadsheet. Studio imports CSV and Excel files with a column mapping step: it reads your headings, works out what each column is and what type it holds, and lets you correct it.

It handles the things that usually break an import — a heading row that is not the first row, mixed date formats, currency symbols, decimal commas — and it tells you exactly what it is about to do before it does it:

5 to add, 0 to update, 0 rows skipped, 0 cells left empty — and then, plainly, 5 added, 0 changed, 40 removed. You see the removals before you agree to them.

You can replace a catalogue outright, or merge a newer price list into the one you already have without doubling anything up.

There is no lock-in. The plain export is a readable document you can keep, archive or move elsewhere. That is deliberate: a format only our software can read would be lock-in wearing a security badge.

The catalogue server

An optional extra for larger fleets. Studio exports a small, ready-to-run program for Windows, Linux or a Raspberry Pi, together with its configuration and its key.

Put it on a machine in your building, and every handset is offered new catalogues over your own network the next time the app is opened on it — no web host, no hosting bill, nothing exposed to the internet.

What it does and refuses

  • The connection is pinned: handsets accept that one machine and no other, so nothing on the network can impersonate it.
  • It refuses any request that does not carry the shared secret it was issued with.
  • It will not serve its own key, will not list a directory, and serves nothing except the catalogue.
  • It mints its own certificate on first run. There is nothing to configure and no certificate to buy.

Once bought, Export catalogue server appears in Studio under Menu ▸ Catalogue. Before it is bought the row is absent rather than greyed out, because a permanently dead control is an advertisement wearing a control's clothes.

Catalogue updates

A catalogue can name an address it updates from — your catalogue server, or any https address you control. Handsets check on their own — but only when somebody opens the app. Nothing runs in the background, so a phone left in a drawer stays on the catalogue it has until it is next opened. Checking more often than every fifteen minutes is ignored.

An update is offered, never applied behind somebody's back. A fetched catalogue goes through the same preview, signature check and confirmation as a file handed over by hand — mid-shift, on somebody's phone, is not the moment to change what the app says.

A source offering an older catalogue than the handset already holds is refused, and the message says who has to act.

Fleet, backup and privacy

Fleet, backup and privacy

Pairing handsets, the recovery file, and what we can and cannot see.

Pairing handsets

Handsets are paired to a Studio in person or over a phone line. Each device shows a code, and you confirm the fingerprint matches — reading it out loud to whoever is holding the other handset is enough. That check is the only thing proving the request came from the device you think it did. From then on that handset trusts catalogues that Studio has signed.

Checking the code is the step that matters: it is the only thing proving the request came from the device in front of you, rather than from somebody else entirely. Several handsets can be paired in one go from a folder of requests, and each one is still confirmed individually.

A handset that is wiped and set up again can be paired again without spending a second slot from your allowance.

The recovery file

A phone is a thing that gets dropped, stolen and left on a roof. So Studio writes a recovery file: one encrypted file holding your Studio identity, your catalogue, the key your fleet trusts, and the photographs and signatures attached to jobs. The catalogue's own part photographs are deliberately not in it — packing every part image into one envelope makes a file too large to be worth having. They travel in a catalogue bundle instead, so keep a bundle beside the recovery file and between them everything is covered.

Restore it on a new phone and that phone is your Studio. The handsets you already paired carry on working, with nothing to redo at their end.

It is encrypted with a passphrase you choose, and we cannot recover it, because we never have it. There is no service, no reset link and no back door. Write the passphrase down somewhere that is not the phone.

Manifest reminds you when there is no recovery file, and tells you how much work is currently held on that handset alone.

Removing a handset

Removing a paired handset frees a slot. It is worth being exact about what it does and does not do, and the app says so on the screen rather than leaving you to find out:

Removing revokes nothing. That handset keeps the fleet key it already has and goes on opening every catalogue it already holds, for ever. There is no way to reach it and no server to tell it otherwise. What removal does is take it off the list, so nothing you seal from now on can be opened by it.

If a handset is genuinely lost or in the wrong hands, take it off the list. That is the step that matters: a sealed export names the handsets paired at the moment it is written, so the missing device is not a recipient of anything you seal afterwards.

Remove and re-key mints a new fleet key and writes a file wrapping it for the handsets you keep. Be clear about what that does today: no handset can load such a file yet, so on its own a re-key shuts nothing out — keep the file with your backups against the day it can. Removal from the list is what changes who can open what.

And nothing stops what is already on that phone being read. Once a file is on a device you cannot reach, no app can reach back — one claiming otherwise would be lying to you.

Copying settings to another device

Destination and source settings can be copied from one device to another — useful when a second person needs the same job delivery set up, or when a Studio is being replaced.

Credentials do not travel. The transfer carries the shape of each destination and leaves its passwords and tokens behind, and the receiving device lists plainly what still needs a credential entering. A configuration file that quietly contained API keys would be the kind of convenience nobody asked for.

What we can see

Nothing.

There is no Manifest account, no Manifest cloud and no Manifest server. We do not receive your catalogue, your jobs, your photographs or your customers' signatures — not because we promise not to look, but because no such destination exists.

  • The database and the photographs on the handset are encrypted at rest, with the key held in the phone's own secure hardware.
  • A catalogue that was not signed by your Studio, or was altered on its way, is refused by a paired handset. Studio authors catalogues, so its own imports are not held to a signature.
  • A plain export has stored credentials stripped out of it, so handing somebody your catalogue does not hand them your API keys. The exception is a secret written into a destination's address, which cannot be removed without breaking the address — Studio warns you where you author one.
  • Studio will not run on a phone with no screen lock.

The full detail, including what a job can contain when your form asks for a name or a signature, is in the privacy policy.

Questions

Questions

The things people reasonably want to know before installing anything.

Can I try it before paying?

Yes. The free tier is a complete Studio on one device — author a catalogue, import your spreadsheet, design job forms, record jobs and preview a label sheet. Four things are paid, and they are the ones that take your work off the handset: pairing other handsets, writing a recovery file, sealing an export, and saving a label sheet as a PDF. Checking and restoring a recovery file are free on every tier, always — the moment you need a backup is the moment you have least.

So you can find out whether the app suits your business before spending anything, and the thing you pay for is putting it in other people's hands.

What happens to my data if I stop using it?

You export it and keep it. A plain export is an ordinary readable document that any tool can open — deliberately, because a format only our software can read would be lock-in wearing a security badge.

Nothing expires. You bought it once; it keeps working offline whatever happens to us.

Does it need the internet at all?

Only for three things, all optional: buying a tier, fetching a catalogue update from an address you configured, and sending completed jobs to a destination you configured.

Everything else — the entire daily use of the app — happens on the handset. A business that hands catalogues around as files and keeps its job sheets on the phone never needs a connection at all.

How big can a catalogue be?

Catalogues of several thousand parts are ordinary, and browsing and search are built to stay quick at that size rather than degrade politely. The practical limit is the phone's storage, and photographs use far more of it than the parts do.

I already have my parts in a spreadsheet

Good — that is the expected starting point. Import it, correct the column mapping, and you have a catalogue. If the spreadsheet is a mess, use the AI conversion schema: send it and your file to any AI assistant and it will produce something importable.

How do I manage users?

You do not, because there are none. A device is the unit of identity: you pair a handset, and that handset can read your catalogues. There is no user directory, no password resets and no leavers process beyond removing the handset.

Jobs can still record who did the work, as a field on the form, because that is a fact about the job rather than a login.

Is there an iPhone version?

Not today. Manifest and Manifest Studio are Android applications.

Something is wrong, or I need a hand

Both apps carry a built-in guide, and Studio can report on the health of your catalogue. For anything else, the contact address is in the privacy policy.

hierarchicalEnumA list with a tree in it, where choosing narrows the next choice.
statusA state with a colour, so it reads at a glance.
tagsFree-form labels, reusable across the catalogue.
ratingA score out of a maximum you set.
colourA colour, shown as a swatch.
emailAn address, checked for shape and tappable to write to.
phoneA number, tappable to dial. Extensions are accepted.
urlA web address, tappable to open.
linkA web address with your own label on it.
personA name — an engineer, a customer, a signatory.
addressA postal address in its proper parts, not one squashed line.
geoPointA latitude and longitude. Shown and sorted; there is no map picker yet, so a value has to arrive in an imported catalogue.
storageLocationWhere the thing physically lives — bay, shelf, van.
serialA serial number, in a monospaced face so 0 and O differ.
barcodeA code that can be printed and scanned back.
nfcTagAn NFC tag identifier, stored and shown as text. The app does not read or write tags itself; the identifier is typed or scanned in.
refA pointer to another part in the same catalogue.
imageA photograph, on a part or on a job.
fileAn attached document — a datasheet, a certificate. No editor yet: a value has to arrive in an imported catalogue.
signatureA signature drawn on the glass, on a job. Once given it cannot be altered, and it is deliberately not a part field.
audioA voice note, recorded through the phone's own recorder. Offered on a job; not yet on a part.
videoA clip, recorded through the phone's own camera app. Offered on a job; not yet on a part.
arrayA repeating list of any of the above.
objectA group of fields treated as one thing.

Glossary

Catalogue

The document holding your fields, structure, appearance and parts. The thing the app takes its shape from.

Part

One record. Depending on your trade that is a stock line, a fitting, or one individually tracked asset.

Build

A group of parts treated as a unit. You can call it a Kit, an Assembly or whatever your trade says.

Plan

A site drawing with zones marked on it and parts placed in them.

Job

One piece of recorded work, filled in on a form you designed.

Destination

Somewhere a completed job is sent — a folder, a web address or an email address.

Studio

The authoring app. One per business, on the phone that owns the catalogue.

Handset

A phone running the reading app, paired to a Studio.

Pairing

The face-to-face step that makes a handset trust your Studio's catalogues.

Recovery file

The encrypted file that lets a replacement phone become your Studio.
